About me

My academic background spans biomedical science, cell biology and biophysical research. After completing my undergraduate and master's degrees in Biomedical Science, I undertook a PhD in Life Sciences at the University of Warwick, where I investigated cellular mechanisms that may contribute to connective tissue dysfunction in Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS) and Hypermobility Spectrum Disorders (HSD).

My journey: from patient to scientist

My interest in this field is a personal one. During my undergraduate studies, I became unwell and was eventually diagnosed with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS). Frustrated by the limited understanding of these conditions and the challenges surrounding their diagnosis, I decided to dedicate my scientific training and academic career to investigating the biological mechanisms that may contribute to EDS and HSD.

My goal has been to help bridge the gap between the lived experience of patients and our understanding of the underlying biology, with the longer-term aim of contributing to improved clinical assessment and the identification of potential diagnostic biomarkers.

Rethinking diagnostics

During my master's degree, I was given the opportunity to undertake independent research into the clinical assessment of hEDS. I focused on the limitations of the Beighton Score, a clinical tool incorporated into the diagnostic criteria for hEDS.

Through a narrative literature review, I identified significant limitations in the use of the Beighton Score, particularly its suitability as a tool for excluding an hEDS diagnosis. This work was subsequently developed with my co-authors, Dr Emma Reinhold and Dr Gemma Pearce, and resulted in my first peer-reviewed publication in Rheumatology International.

Following its publication, the Ehlers-Danlos Society acknowledged the limitations of the Beighton Score when announcing its review of the hEDS diagnostic criteria. This experience reinforced my interest in addressing gaps between clinical practice, scientific evidence and the experiences of patients.

Pioneering a mechanobiological approach to EDS and HSD

For my PhD, I independently developed a novel hypothesis proposing that cellular mechanobiology could contribute to the pathogenesis of EDS and HSD. At the time, previous studies had identified abnormalities in cell adhesion and integrin expression in EDS fibroblasts, but their pathological significance remained unclear.

I brought these findings together with emerging research in fibroblast mechanobiology to develop the first explicit mechanobiological framework linking altered cell-matrix interactions, cytoskeletal dynamics and cellular force generation to the mechanical integrity of connective tissue. In this model, changes in the way fibroblasts adhere to and mechanically interact with the extracellular matrix (ECM) could alter cell behaviour and, ultimately, contribute to abnormal connective tissue properties.

With the support of my supervisors, Dr Darius Koster and Dr Wing Ying Chow, I secured funding from the Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC) through the Midlands Integrative Biosciences Training Partnership (MIBTP) to investigate this hypothesis.

My 2021 literature review formalised this proposed pathomechanism and established a new direction for mechanobiological research in EDS/HSD. The framework brought together previously disparate observations of altered integrin expression, cytoskeletal organisation and cell biomechanics, proposing how these cellular changes could contribute to the mechanical abnormalities of connective tissue in EDS/HSD. This work formed the basis of my second peer-reviewed publication in Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology.

The subsequent experimental phase of my research investigated this pathomechanism using advanced cellular and biophysical approaches. I developed an in vitro model in which healthy and EDS fibroblasts produce their own native ECM. Using this system, I investigated changes in ECM organisation and fibroblast behaviour across multiple time points, combining advanced structural imaging of the ECM with immunofluorescence analysis of cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organisation and contractile phenotype. Unlike previous studies that examined these cellular features in short-term cultures without a cell-derived matrix, my approach enables fibroblast behaviour to be studied in a more physiologically relevant, matrix-supporting environment. This work provides a means of investigating how the extracellular environment influences fibroblast adhesion and contractility in EDS.

I have now completed my PhD and subsequent postdoctoral research investigating the mechanisms proposed by this research programme. I am currently finalising a research paper based on my experimental work at the University of Warwick.

What began as a hypothesis developed through a literature review has since grown into an active area of research spanning two research laboratories at the University of Warwick. I hope to continue developing this work to better understand the biological basis of EDS and HSD and, ultimately, to contribute to improved approaches to diagnosis and patient care.

Public engagement and co-creation

I have a longstanding interest in making biomedical research accessible beyond academia and ensuring that research addresses the needs of the communities it seeks to benefit. During a professional internship with hEDStogether in 2020, I contributed to public engagement around hEDS/HSD, developed accessible educational content and gained experience in research communication and project coordination. I also contributed as a co-creator to a project developing educational resources for maternity care in hEDS/HSD.

I believe that meaningful progress in EDS and HSD research requires dialogue between researchers, clinicians and patients. By bringing together rigorous scientific investigation with an understanding of the lived experience of these conditions, my aim is to help advance research that is both scientifically meaningful and clinically relevant.
